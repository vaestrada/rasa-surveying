The finding that shaped all five

The Projects page has no projects on it.

rasasurvey.com lists ten project categories — airport and port, hydropower, pipeline, transmission line, mining, parcellary — and not a single project inside them. No client names, no dates, no scope, no numbers. The Technology page carries one instrument.

Thirty years of surveying the country's expressways, bridges, dams and grid corridors, and almost none of it is on the web. That is not a design problem. It is the most valuable unbuilt asset in the business, and it is why a procurement officer comparing three firms cannot currently choose RASA on evidence.

How to read these

Two decisions, not one.

Most website reviews collapse into taste. This one shouldn't. There are two separate questions and they have different right answers.

Decision A · Strategy

What is the site for?

Winning tenders? Selling equipment? Proving national coverage? Fixing the content bottleneck? This is a business decision, and it belongs to RASA. The five concepts each answer it differently.

Decision B · Style

What should it look like?

Colour, type, imagery, motion. Easy to change later and cheap to iterate. Do not let this drive Decision A — a direction that looks good but serves the wrong audience is the expensive mistake.

Practical note: it is completely legitimate to say "the strategy of 04, the look of 02." That is a useful answer. What is not useful is "the header of 02 with the colours of 04 and the layout of 01" — that produces a website nobody designed.

The thing worth understanding before you choose

"Generic" is a content problem, not a layout problem.

Look at any well-made contractor or engineering website and you will find the same skeleton: photo hero with two buttons, icon trust strip, sectors, services grid, featured projects, numbered process, testimonials, FAQ, fat footer. It repeats because it works — it is the path a B2B buyer expects to walk, and deviating from it costs trust rather than earning attention.

What makes those sites feel interchangeable is not the skeleton. It is that the contents of the slots are fungible — "450+ projects", "98% satisfaction", "Quality Craftsmanship" could belong to any company in any country.

Put CLLEX, the Bataan–Cavite Interlink Bridge, the RIEGL miniVUX-1UAV, PRS92 and ±7 cm into those same slots and the identical layout stops being generic. Conventional structure, specific substance. That is what Concept 06 is, and it is why it is the recommendation.

The decision instrument

Don't ask them which one they like.

"Which do you prefer?" produces taste, hedging, and a Frankenstein brief. Ask these four instead — they produce a decision.

Question 01

Where does next year's revenue actually come from — services, or equipment?

If services and public bidding, the answer is 02 or 03. If equipment sales, it's 04. This single question eliminates three of the five.

Question 02

Picture the person you most want on this site. Who are they?

A DPWH shortlisting officer, a private consultant's project engineer, a buyer comparing GNSS receivers, or a graduate geodetic engineer looking for a job. Each points somewhere different.

Question 03

Can you supply the project records — client, year, scope, instrument?

02 and 03 are the strongest concepts, and both are worthless without this data. If it can't be assembled, say so now and the choice narrows honestly.

Question 04

Is the content bottleneck a website problem or a staffing problem?

If the team will keep producing weekly regardless, 05 multiplies their output. If nobody will feed it, 05 becomes an empty magazine — the worst outcome on this page.

Architecture

One site, four companies — solved five ways.

RASA owns all four and wants one site to maintain. That is the right call, and it is a design problem worth deciding deliberately rather than defaulting to tabs.

The options on the table

01 — Persistent tab switcher. Four tabs in the utility bar, always visible. Familiar, cheap, slightly bureaucratic.
02 — Map layers. Each company is a layer you toggle. Elegant, and it makes the group feel like one system rather than four brochures.
03 — Formal divisions. A group masthead with four numbered divisions. Reads as a serious holding company.
04 — Audience-first. The visitor picks what they need; the site routes to the right company invisibly. Best for conversion, hardest to build.
05 — Editorial desks. Four desks under one masthead. Strong if the site publishes; meaningless if it doesn't.
